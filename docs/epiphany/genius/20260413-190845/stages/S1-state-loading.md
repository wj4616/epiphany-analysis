## Category 1 — Core Claim / Question

What is the central thing being asked or asserted?

1. Pre-Mortem is a technique where teams imagine a project has already failed, then reason backward to identify causes.
2. Prospective hindsight (imagining a future event has already occurred) increases cause-identification ability by 30% (Gary Klein research).
3. The central mechanism is framing failure as certain, not possible.
4. This certainty framing bypasses optimism bias.
5. The technique is categorized as a retrospective despite occurring before project completion.
6. The task asks for cognitive enhancement analysis of the guide itself — not the projects the guide describes.
7. The guide claims Pre-Mortem produces "deeper insights" vs. traditional risk planning.
8. The guide positions Pre-Mortem as psychologically safer than criticizing current plans.
9. The guide presents Pre-Mortem as a team-based process (4–12 people), not individual.
10. The technique requires explicit facilitation — it is not self-executing.
11. The guide's key prompt uses strong, definitive past-tense framing: "The project has completely failed."
12. Prevention strategies are the primary output, not failure identification per se.
13. The guide claims Pre-Mortem surfaces risks that wouldn't otherwise emerge.
14. The guide positions ownership assignment (Step 6) as the terminal deliverable.
15. Four variations are described: Speed, Category, Optimistic (Pre-Parade), Rolling.
16. The guide treats Pre-Mortem and Traditional Risk Assessment as distinct (story-based vs. list-based).

## Category 2 — Background Facts

What is established about the topic?

1. Gary Klein popularized the Pre-Mortem technique.
2. Prospective hindsight = imagining a future event has already occurred.
3. Klein's research: prospective hindsight increases reason-identification by 30%.
4. Pre-Mortem timing: before or during project (not after).
5. Post-Mortem timing: after project or incident.
6. Pre-Mortem emotional state: low stakes (hypothetical).
7. Post-Mortem emotional state: high stakes (real impact).
8. Optimism bias: teams naturally focus on how things will succeed.
9. Futurespective is the optimistic counterpart to Pre-Mortem.
10. Traditional Risk Assessment is list-based; Pre-Mortem is story-based.
11. Recommended team size: 4–12 people.
12. Any experience level can participate.
13. Session duration: 45–60 minutes.
14. Four common failure categories: Technical, People, Process, External.
15. The technique works best at: Project Kickoffs, Mid-Project Check-ins, Before Major Releases, Sprint Planning, High-Stakes Decisions.
16. Individual brainstorming precedes group sharing (Step 2 before Step 3) — this prevents anchoring/groupthink.
17. Cards (one reason per card) are the artifact of Step 2.
18. Voting is used for prioritization (Step 4), targeting top 5–7 failures.
19. Each prevention strategy requires an owner, timeline, and success measure (Step 6).
20. Speed Pre-Mortem variant: 20 minutes.
21. Pre-Parade variant: imagines spectacular success instead of failure.
22. Rolling Pre-Mortem: repeated at multiple project stages.
23. Category Pre-Mortem: focuses on one failure domain at a time.

## Category 3 — Key Tensions

Where do competing considerations pull in different directions?

1. Certainty framing vs. believability: "The project has completely failed" is powerful but may strain credulity on low-risk projects, reducing engagement.
2. Psychological safety vs. group dynamics: the technique creates safety to voice concerns, but the same group dynamics (hierarchy, politics) that suppress concerns in normal meetings are still present during the Pre-Mortem.
3. Individual brainstorming vs. group convergence: Step 2 isolates individuals to avoid anchoring, but Step 3 groups similar items, which re-introduces convergence pressure.
4. Voting as prioritization vs. voting as social influence: dot voting surfaces the most salient/feared risks, not necessarily the most likely or most impactful — these can diverge significantly.
5. Prevention focus vs. risk acceptance: not all identified risks should be prevented; some should be accepted, transferred, or monitored, but the guide frames all outputs as prevention strategies.
6. Facilitation quality vs. technique robustness: the effectiveness of the technique depends heavily on facilitator skill, which is underspecified in the guide.
7. Pre-Mortem as prevention vs. Pre-Mortem as political cover: teams can run Pre-Mortems to document that risks were identified without genuinely acting on them.
8. Ownership assignment (Step 6) vs. authority to act: assigning an owner without commensurate authority creates accountability without agency.
9. Low-stakes hypothetical framing vs. organizational memory: if the Pre-Mortem outputs are archived and referenced later, the "low stakes" nature evaporates — people may self-censor knowing outputs will be reviewed.
10. 30% improvement claim vs. base rate ambiguity: the 30% figure describes reason-identification, not prevention effectiveness — the guide elides this distinction.
11. Inclusivity (4–12 people, any experience level) vs. depth: broader participation surfaces more risks but reduces time per risk for analysis.
12. Time constraint (45–60 min) vs. genuine analysis: six structured steps in 45 minutes allows approximately 7–10 minutes per step — insufficient for complex projects.
13. Rolling Pre-Mortem variant vs. habituation: repeating the technique may reduce its psychological novelty, weakening the failure-as-certain framing effect.
14. Pre-Mortem vs. Futurespective: the guide presents these as distinct tools, but the Pre-Parade variant blurs this boundary, suggesting the framing is a dial, not a binary.
15. Top 5–7 prioritized failures vs. long-tail risks: the prioritization step discards the lower-priority items entirely; those items may represent systemic risks that individually seem unlikely but collectively matter.
16. "Wild scenarios" encouraged by facilitator vs. anchoring to realistic concerns: if wild scenarios dominate, resources may be allocated to low-probability risks.
17. Step 6 (ownership) as closure vs. false confidence: assigning owners and timelines creates a sense of completion that may reduce vigilance during actual execution.
18. Pre-Mortem benefits described are psychological (safety, permission) vs. the output is structural (prevention strategies) — it is unclear whether the psychological mechanism reliably produces structural quality.
19. Technique applicability claimed broadly (any project, any experience level) vs. context-dependence: the technique's power likely varies significantly by domain, culture, and project type.
20. The guide's framing of Pre-Mortem as "unlocking deeper insights" is unverified — it asserts mechanism (optimism bias bypass) without evidence that the resulting prevention strategies actually reduce failure rates.

## Category 4 — Stakeholders

Who is affected by the answer? What do they value?

1. Project teams (primary users): value risk reduction, psychological safety, efficient time use.
2. Project managers / facilitators: value structured process, clear facilitation guide, measurable output.
3. Organizational leadership / sponsors: value project success rate, ROI on prevention activities, accountability.
4. Individual team members without authority: value psychological safety to voice concerns without career risk.
5. Senior or dominant team members: may resist equal-weight brainstorming; value hierarchy-aware risk framing.
6. External stakeholders (customers, partners): affected by project outcomes but absent from the Pre-Mortem.
7. Risk management teams: value integration with existing risk frameworks; may resist parallel unaligned process.
8. Agile coaches / scrum masters: value ceremonies that fit sprint cadence; Rolling Pre-Mortem maps to this.
9. Teams in high-blame cultures: the technique's psychological safety claim may be entirely undermined; they are adversely affected if the technique is applied naively.
10. Distributed/remote teams: asynchronous card-writing variant is not described; Step 2 assumes co-location or synchronous virtual participation.
11. Researchers / practitioners who study retrospective effectiveness: value evidence-based claims; the guide's 30% figure has limited provenance as cited.
12. The guide's author / publisher: values adoption, credibility, and practical utility.

## Category 7 — Open Sub-Questions

What questions must be answered before the main question can be answered?

1. Does the 30% improvement in reason-identification (Klein) translate into measurable reduction in project failure rates? (Mechanism ≠ outcome.)
2. Under what cultural conditions does psychological safety actually emerge from Pre-Mortem framing vs. being suppressed by organizational dynamics?
3. Is dot voting a valid prioritization mechanism for risk, or does it primarily surface the most emotionally salient risks?
4. What distinguishes a Pre-Mortem that produces genuine prevention strategy from one that produces performative documentation?
5. How does the technique's effectiveness vary by project type (software vs. hardware vs. organizational change vs. creative)?
6. What is the optimal team composition for a Pre-Mortem — does role diversity matter, and how?
7. Does the Rolling Pre-Mortem variant preserve effectiveness over time, or does habituation degrade the failure-as-certain framing?
8. What happens to the lower-priority failures discarded in Step 4? Is there a long-tail risk aggregation problem?
9. How should prevention strategies be differentiated from risk acceptance, risk transfer, and monitoring — distinctions the guide collapses?
10. What is the accountability mechanism between Step 6 (ownership assignment) and actual execution — and what are failure modes of this handoff?
11. Can the technique be adapted for asynchronous or distributed teams without losing the psychological mechanism?
12. Is the individual card-writing in Step 2 actually sufficient to prevent anchoring, or does prior group discussion (e.g., project kickoff) already introduce anchoring?
13. How does the Pre-Mortem interact with existing risk registers and risk frameworks — does it complement, duplicate, or conflict?
14. What are the ethical risks of using Pre-Mortem outputs as retrospective accountability evidence?
15. Is the 45–60 minute time frame evidence-based, or is it a practical concession that trades depth for accessibility?
16. What is the failure rate of Pre-Mortems themselves — sessions that produce no actionable prevention or whose prevention strategies are never implemented?
17. Does the Pre-Parade (optimistic) variant produce different or complementary insights — and should both be run together as standard practice?
18. What cognitive biases does the Pre-Mortem technique itself introduce or amplify (e.g., availability bias in failure scenario generation)?
19. Can a single facilitator effectively manage both the psychological safety creation and the content facilitation simultaneously?
20. What is the relationship between Pre-Mortem quality and team psychological safety pre-existing the session — does baseline safety moderate effectiveness?

## Simplicity Stripping Applied

**Scaffolding removed:** framing language from the guide ("That's the power of...", "This guide shows you..."), transitional section headers used as content, meta-commentary about the technique's branding.

**Dependency chains identified and struck:**

- "Bypasses optimism bias" [Category 1, item 4] → derives from items 1+3 (certainty framing is the mechanism; optimism-bias bypass is the stated consequence). Strike as derivative; keep the mechanism (certainty framing) as the irreducible.
- "Creates psychological safety" [guide claim] → partially derives from certainty framing (low-stakes hypothetical). Not fully derivable — organizational culture is an independent variable. Keep as conditional irreducible with the culture caveat.
- "Drives proactive action" [guide claim] → derives from "prevention strategies are the output" (Category 1, item 12). Strike as derivable; keep the structural output claim.
- "Individual card-writing before group sharing" [Category 2, item 16] → the anchoring-prevention rationale derives from this structural fact. Keep structure; note the mechanism is inferred, not explicitly stated in the guide.
- "Voting targets top 5–7 failures" [Category 2, item 18] → derives from Step 4 description. The specific number (5–7) is an irreducible bound claim; keep.
- "Rolling Pre-Mortem may suffer habituation" [Category 3, item 13] → derives from the general psychological principle that novelty effects degrade with repetition, not from the input text. Mark as assumption introduced by analysis, not input fact.

**Irreducibles surviving stripping (consolidated):**

1. Mechanism: prospective hindsight (imagining failure has occurred) → 30% increase in reason-identification (Klein, cited).
2. Critical elision: 30% improvement is in reason-identification, not in prevention effectiveness or project success rate.
3. Structural constraint: certainty framing ("has failed") is the load-bearing element — weakening it (e.g., "might fail") likely degrades effectiveness.
4. Individual isolation in Step 2 is the anchoring-prevention mechanism; Step 3 group convergence partially re-introduces groupthink pressure.
5. Voting as prioritization is a social salience filter, not a probability × impact filter.
6. Ownership assignment (Step 6) creates accountability without verifying authority to act.
7. Psychological safety is assumed as an output of the technique; in high-blame cultures, it may not emerge.
8. The guide collapses risk response strategies: all identified risks are treated as prevention targets; acceptance/transfer/monitoring are absent.
9. Facilitator skill is a critical but underspecified dependency.
10. Time constraint (45–60 min for 6 steps) structurally limits depth of prevention strategy development.
11. External stakeholders (customers, partners, regulators) are systematically absent from the technique as described.
12. The guide presents no measurement framework for Pre-Mortem effectiveness — there is no way to know if a given session was good.
13. The Pre-Parade variant inverts the framing but shares the same structural steps — suggesting the failure/success framing is a dial, not binary, which the guide does not acknowledge.
14. The technique produces no mechanism for aggregating or acting on the lower-priority items discarded in Step 4.
15. "No derivation chains found" for the analogous domains (Category 5) and open sub-questions (Category 7) — all primitives in those categories are irreducible as stated.
